Things I Learned in 2024

01 Jan, 2024 · 15 min read

  1. Bronze Age iron artifacts likely originated from meteorites, not from smelting terrestrial iron 🏆☄️. A geochemical approach using Fe:Co:Ni ratios can distinguish terrestrial from extraterrestrial iron. Modern iron and iron ores have distinct Fe:Co:Ni ratios compared to those found in Bronze Age artifacts.

  2. The U.S. never adopted the metric system due to Caribbean pirates 📐🏴‍☠️. When the U.S. was considering standardizing measurements, Thomas Jefferson arranged for French scientist Joseph Dombey to deliver a kilogram standard. British privateers intercepted Dombey in the Caribbean, leading to his capture and eventual death. The kilogram standard reached the U.S. too late to influence the metric system’s adoption.

  3. Any Rubik’s Cube can be solved in 20 moves or less 🧊🤔. Researchers computed all possible cube positions, using about 35 CPU-years of computing time donated by Google.

  4. The Kellogg’s brand has split in 2023, forming WK Kellogg Co for its North American cereal division and rebranding the remaining operations to Kellanova 🥣🍿💔. BTW: Big Snack is a fascinating rabbit hole to go down. For example:

    • Axe or Lynx, launched in 1983, is a French brand owned by Unilever

  5. Speaking of snacks: Most potato chips are seasoned by a select few seasoning houses 🥔🧂. These seasoning houses develop new flavors for global markets. They work closely with big snack brands, but stay low-profile to protect proprietary formulas and keep competitors guessing.

  6. Tyromancy is the art of fortune-telling with cheese 🧙‍♂️🔮🧀.

    Back then, a typical use for tyromancy was to determine who you would marry. You’d simply carve the names of all potential suitors into some pieces of cheese, then wait to see which one molded first. And there it was—your life partner! People also analyzed the number and size of holes in a block of cheese, the patterns of the mold and veins, and the shapes that curds made as they coagulated.

  7. Recent studies using data from the Hubble Space Telescope and the European Southern Observatory have updated the perceived colors of Neptune and Uranus, showing both planets share a similar shade of greenish-blue 🪐. This challenges the long-held belief that Neptune was deep blue and Uranus pale cyan. That earlier impression came from poorly balanced composite images from the 20th century, including Voyager 2 imagery.

  8. Most people can intuitively understand chicken emotions 🐓. Approximately 70% of study participants could accurately interpret excitement or discontent from chicken clucks, regardless of their experience with chickens.

  9. The ancient Phoenician city of Byblos is the origin of the terms book and bible 📖. Byblos was renowned for exporting papyrus. This fed into the Greek “biblos” (papyrus/scroll), which became “biblion” (little scroll) and then the plural “biblia” (books). In Latin, “biblia” eventually got treated as singular, meaning the scriptures as one unified Book.

  10. God rays or crepuscular rays are sunbeams visible when the Sun is near the horizon, obscured by clouds, during twilight. A related phenomenon is anticrepuscular rays, appearing in the opposite direction of the setting or rising sun 🌄🌅

  11. Wikipedia has a list of tautological place names – places where different parts of the name mean the same thing 🗣🗣.

    • Lake Baikal, Russia (‘Lake Rich lake’ – From the Turkic baj (rich, though this is disputed) and kal (lake).
    • Mekong River - A triple tautonym. ‘Mae’ in Thai is an abbreviation for “river”, while ‘khong’ is an old Austroasiatic word for river. Mekong River can thus be translated as “river river river”

  12. The US has more golf courses than McDonald’s locations ⛳🏌️‍♂️🍟🍔. As of 2024, there are about 16,000 golf courses and about 13,000 McDonald’s locations in the United States. McDonald’s is heavily concentrated in urban areas (highest density in East Los Angeles and just outside Chicago). Golf courses are more evenly distributed, including in less populated areas, especially in the Midwest. (Via Kottke)

  13. 150 of the world’s 700 Seke speakers live in two buildings in Brooklyn 🏙.. The Endangered Language Alliance, dedicated to preserving languages spoken by small populations, notes that New York City is home to more than 700 languages, with over 150 at significant risk of extinction. About 150 Seke speakers, originally from a handful of villages in Nepal, live in two adjacent apartment buildings in Brooklyn. (Via Neatorama)

  14. The ‘bite’ in Apple’s logo was designed to match the curvature of the Motter Tektura typeface’s lowercase “a” 🍎. The Motter Tektura typeface was used by Apple in the late 1970s and into the 1980s. It was designed by Othmar Motter of Vorarlberger Graphik in 1975 and was part of Apple’s branding and was utilized in various promotional materials, packaging, and product labeling.

  15. Before 1971, one British pound was divided into 20 shillings, and one shilling was equal to 12 pence (so there were 240 pence in a pound) 💷. After decimalization in 1971, one pound was divided into 100 pence. The shilling stayed in circulation until 1990, and after decimalization it was worth five new pence.

  16. A snow leopard is not a species of leopard ❄️🐆. The snow leopard (Panthera uncia, formerly Uncia uncia) and the leopard (Panthera pardus) are distinct species within the Panthera genus (alongside lions, tigers, and jaguars). Genetic studies suggest snow leopards are more closely related to tigers (Panthera tigris) than to leopards.

  17. Margarine was invented in 1869 🧈.

    Napoleon III had offered a prize to the person who could devise “a substitute product for ordinary butter, cheaper and which keeps well, for the navy and the less well-off classes,” which Hippolyte Mège-Mouriès, a French chemist, won with a mixture of tallow, water, skim milk, and sodium bicarbonate.

    Mège-Mouriès named his creation oleomargarine, the “margarine” being derived from the Greek word for “pearl.”

    Mouriès licensed his invention to Jurgens, the Dutch company that would, several mergers later, put the Uni in Unilever.

  23. Earthquakes on the U.S. East Coast are felt farther than ones on the West Coast The reason? East of the Rocky Mountains, much of the rocky underground is hundreds of millions of years older than the subterranean formations to the west. Older rocks tend to be harder and denser, and older fault lines have had more time to “heal.” All of that means seismic waves travel more efficiently across the eastern U.S. The reverse is true in the West, where younger faults absorb more of the wave energy, so it doesn’t spread as far. This not only explains why a California quake is felt in a smaller area, but also why this more focused shaking can cause much more damage.

  25. The ability to see “red” wavelengths might have developed by duplicating and mutating the genes responsible for seeing green 💚🧬❤️ This mechanism has been shown in some jumping spiders, where an opsin gene is duplicated, followed by functional divergence. It evolved independently, multiple times, in different jumping spider lineages and is asserted to have occurred in primates.

  28. Elephants have names for each other 🐘 Unlike dolphins and parrots,

    While dolphins and parrots address conspecifics by imitating the calls of the addressee, human names are not imitations of the sounds typically made by the named individual. Labelling objects or individuals without relying on imitation of the sounds made by the referent radically expands the expressive power of language. Thus, if non-imitative name analogues were found in other species, this could have important implications for our understanding of language evolution. Here we present evidence that wild African elephants address one another with individually specific calls, probably without relying on imitation of the receiver.

    Moreover, elephants differentially responded to playbacks of calls originally addressed to them relative to calls addressed to a different individual. (Via Yale School of the Environment)

  30. Walnut derives from “nut of the speaker of a Romance language” 🌰 Originally this referred to Celts; later, it referred to Latin/Romance speakers, who introduced the nut to Germanic-speaking regions from areas under Roman cultural and linguistic influence.

  34. Trees exposed to wildfire smoke “hold their breath" 💨🌲 Dense wildfire smoke triggers stomatal closure, effectively halting gas exchange between the leaf interior and the atmosphere.

  35. Cats have vertical pupils because they help with low-light conditions and enable high precision in judging prey distance 🐈👁️ Vertical pupils can expand and contract over a very large range (up to ~135× area change), allowing effective vision from bright daylight to near darkness. They also enhance depth perception via blur gradients. Blur is preserved primarily along horizontal contours (e.g., the ground), leading to a strong gradient of defocus with distance, which small, ground-level predators can exploit to estimate prey distance.

  38. Did you ever read about a topic you’re an expert in and think ’this isn’t right’? 🤔

    “Gell-Mann amnesia effect” describes the phenomenon of experts reading articles within their fields of expertise and finding them to be error-ridden and full of misunderstanding, but seemingly forgetting those experiences when reading articles in the same publications written on topics outside of their fields of expertise, which they believe to be credible.

  39. ‘The data on extreme human aging is rotten from the inside out’ The 2024 Ig Nobel Prize in Demography goes to

    Saul Justin Newman, for detective work in discovering that many of the people famous for having the longest lives lived in places that had lousy birth-and-death recordkeeping.

    Here is Newman himself:

    I’ve tracked down 80% of the people aged over 110 in the world (the other 20% are from countries you can’t meaningfully analyse). Of those, almost none have a birth certificate. In the US there are over 500 of these people; seven have a birth certificate. Even worse, only about 10% have a death certificate.

    The epitome of this is blue zones, which are regions where people supposedly reach age 100 at a remarkable rate. For almost 20 years, they have been marketed to the public.

    Okinawa in Japan is one of these zones. There was a Japanese government review in 2010, which found that 82% of the people aged over 100 in Japan turned out to be dead. The secret to living to 110 was, don’t register your death.

  40. Brother, the printer company, was originally founded as a sewing machine manufacturer 🪡. It was founded in 1908 when it was originally called Yasui Sewing Machine Co in Nagoya, Japan. Though mostly known for its printers, nowadays, Brother still has a fairly large sewing machine division.

  41. The word escalate was coined by the Escalator, not the other way around 🛗. Escalator was originally trademarked by the Otis company. Inventor Charles Seeberger coined the word from scala, Latin for “steps,” to express “move using steps,” as opposed to the “elevator,” which raises things to another level.

  42. Red Pandas Aren’t Named After Pandas 🐼

    People were calling red pandas (Ailurus fulgens) “pandas” since early in the 19th century, some 40 years before anyone called the bears “pandas.”

    The bears that we now call pandas were then named “giant pandas”. Over time, as the giant pandas became increasingly famous, Ailurus fulgens became known as “lesser pandas,” then red pandas. Finally, giant pandas became commonly known simply as “pandas.”

    (Via Cracked)
